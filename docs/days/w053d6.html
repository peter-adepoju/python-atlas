Week 53, Day 6Forge day

Forge

This week turned dynamic programming into a method: spot optimal substructure and overlapping subproblems, design the state, write the recurrence, then memoise it or fill a table in the right order, roll it to save memory, and walk back to recover the choices. You met the one-dimensional patterns, grid DP and the knapsack family. Today’s Forge is a festival planner that books acts for one stage on a budget, which turns out to be a knapsack and a weighted interval schedule at once. The bonus is a climbing wall: grid DP with an extra counter in the state. The weekly quiz and review finish the week.

By the end of today you can

  • Combine a knapsack budget with weighted interval scheduling in one table
  • Reconstruct a choice that jumps back to an earlier compatible row
  • Swap the roles of budget and value to find the least cost for a target
  • Add a counter to a grid DP’s state and count routes with the same table shape

Mixed review

Exercise10 points
The best stretch of a ring

Numbers stand round a circle. Write best_circular(nums): the largest sum of a non-empty run of neighbouring numbers, where a run may wrap from the end back to the start. Use Kadane twice: the best run that doesn’t wrap, and the total minus the smallest run (which is what a wrapping run leaves out). Take care when every number is negative: then the “wrapping” answer would be the empty run.

Exercise10 points
Painting the fence

A fence has n posts, each painted one of k colours, with no three posts in a row the same colour. Write paint_ways(n, k) with two rolling values: the ways ending in two posts of the same colour, and the ways ending in a post different from the one before.

Forge: a festival planner

A town festival has one stage and a budget for fees. Each act has a fee, an expected crowd, and a time slot from start to end (whole hours; an act ending at 14 and one starting at 14 don’t clash). The organisers want the biggest total crowd from acts that don’t overlap, within the budget.

It’s two of this week’s ideas at once. Sort acts by end time: the weighted interval schedule (Week 52 Day 2’s challenge) says f(i) chooses between leaving act i out and taking it, which jumps back to the last act that ends by its start. The budget adds the knapsack’s second dimension.

Write FestivalPlanner(acts, budget), where acts maps a name to (fee, crowd, start, end):

  • Raise ValueError for a negative budget, fee or crowd, or an act whose end isn’t after its start.
  • best_lineup(): (crowd, names), the largest total crowd from non-overlapping acts whose fees fit the budget, with the names in order of start time. Use a table best(i, b) over the first i acts in end order and budget b, with bisect to find how many acts end by each act’s start; walk back to find the acts.
  • best_unlimited(): the largest crowd ignoring the budget (one-dimensional).
  • cheapest_for(target): the least total fee for non-overlapping acts with a total crowd of at least target, or None. Swap the roles: a table of the least fee over (acts considered, crowd still needed), where the crowd still needed never drops below 0.
  • count_lineups(): how many sets of non-overlapping acts (including the empty set) fit the budget.

Forge: a festival planner

Write FestivalPlanner to the specification above.

Two dimensions from two constraints

The festival table has one dimension for each thing the question constrains: which acts have been considered (because of the time slots) and how much budget is left. That’s the general rule for designing a DP state when a problem has several limits: each independent limit becomes a coordinate, and the table’s size is the product of their ranges. It’s why a third limit (say, a cap on the number of acts) is affordable when its range is small, and why limits measured in millions make DP tables impossible, sending you back to last week’s branch and bound.

Forge bonus: a climbing wall

A climbing wall is a grid of holds, given as strings from the top row to the bottom: a digit is the effort of using that hold, and # is no hold. A climber starts on any hold in the bottom row and moves up one row at a time, to the hold straight above or diagonally up-left or up-right, until reaching the top row. Every hold used, including the first and last, adds its effort. Write ClimbingWall(rows):

  • Raise ValueError if the rows are empty, of different lengths, or contain anything but digits and #.
  • easiest(max_sideways=None): the least total effort to climb the wall, or None if it can’t be climbed. With max_sideways, at most that many of the moves may be diagonal; add the number of diagonal moves used to the state.
  • route(): the columns of the holds on one easiest climb, from the bottom row to the top, or None.
  • routes(): the number of different climbs, using one row of counts.
