Week 87, Day 1

Choosing a capstone

Phase 7 begins with the biggest piece of work in the course: a capstone, a project of your own, built over six weeks from an idea to a release you can show anyone. Everything since Week 1 goes into it. This week is about starting well, because most projects that fail were the wrong size or the wrong idea from the first day. Today you’ll find ideas, judge them on purpose rather than on mood, estimate how big they are, cut them down to something you can finish, name the risks, and write a pitch. You’ll do all of it twice: once for a worked example the course builds alongside you, Waymark, a planner for walks, and once for your own capstone, on your own computer.

By the end of today you can

  • Explain what a capstone is for, and recognise ideas that are too big, too vague or too risky
  • Compare ideas with a weighted scoring matrix and dealbreakers
  • Estimate work with three-point estimates, and judge a plan’s chance of fitting the time you have
  • Cut a project to a minimum version with MoSCoW priorities, and rank its risks
  • Write a short, concrete pitch

Unweighted, the chess engine and the recipe box tie on 12 and the planner wins narrowly. Weighting what matters most for this project (being able to finish it) changes the gaps. Choosing an idea is a decision like any other: write down what matters, score honestly, and let the numbers start the argument rather than end it.

What a capstone is for

A capstone does three jobs at once:

  • It proves you can build something whole. Not an exercise with checks waiting for it, but a program with a purpose, decisions, tests, documentation and a release: the whole of Phase 6 in one place.
  • It’s the centrepiece of your portfolio. Someone deciding whether to work with you will look at one project properly. A finished, well-explained small project beats an impressive unfinished one every time.
  • It teaches what exercises can’t: scoping, changing your mind, debugging your own design, and finishing.

The six weeks are planned for you: this week, specification (an idea, a written spec, an architecture, a repository with CI, and a first thin slice working end to end); three build sprints; a week to harden it (performance, security, errors and logging, documentation, testing with real users); and a week to ship (a release, a write-up, a demo, a portfolio page). Plan on about 10 to 12 hours a week of work on it.

The worked example. Alongside your own project, the course builds one in the browser, so every step has an example you can run and check: Waymark, a planner for walks. It holds a network of footpaths (junctions, paths between them, their length and climb), finds routes between places, estimates how long they’ll take, saves favourite walks and keeps a log of walks done, with a small API and a command-line tool. It touches most of the course: classes, graphs and shortest paths, a database, an API, tests and data.

Finding and judging ideas

Good capstone ideas usually come from your own life: something you or people you know do by hand, a hobby with data in it, a club that runs on spreadsheets, a tool you wished you’d had earlier in this course. Those come with real users and real data, and you’ll know when the result is right. Extending an earlier project (the spend tracker, the shelf library, the helpdesk) is fine too, if the extension is substantial.

Be wary of ideas that are:

  • Too big: “a social network”, “a game engine”, “my own programming language”. Six weeks is about 70 hours.
  • Too vague: “something with machine learning”. An idea is a user, a problem and a thing that solves it.
  • Dependent on what you can’t control: data you can’t get, an API that may change or charge, other people’s time.
  • A copy of a large existing product: the interesting parts are the bits a clone leaves out.

To choose between a few candidates, use a weighted scoring matrix: pick criteria (how interested you are, how useful it would be, which skills it shows, whether you can finish it, whether you can get the data), weight them by how much they matter, and score each idea from 1 to 5. Some criteria are dealbreakers: a 1 for feasibility or data rules an idea out whatever its total.

How big is it?

People underestimate work, by a lot and consistently. Two habits help:

  • Three-point estimates. For each piece of work, estimate an optimistic time o, a most likely time m and a pessimistic time p. The PERT estimate is (o + 4m + p) / 6, with a standard deviation of about (p − o) / 6. Over many tasks, the expected times add up, but the spreads don’t: the total’s standard deviation is the square root of the sum of the variances (each task’s spread squared), because some tasks finish early while others run late.
  • Capacity, not calendar. Ten hours a week for six weeks isn’t sixty hours of building: meetings with yourself, reading documentation, being stuck and tidying up take a share. A focus factor of about 0.7 is realistic.

Then cut. Sort features with MoSCoW: Must have (without it, it isn’t the product), Should have (important but it still works without), Could have (nice extras), Won’t have this time (written down so you stop thinking about them). The minimum viable product (MVP) is the musts: the smallest version that’s useful end to end. If the musts don’t fit your capacity, the idea is too big: cut it, don’t hope.

Risks and the pitch

A risk is something that might go wrong and would hurt if it did: “I can’t get footpath data”, “the route search is too slow for a big map”, “I lose two weeks to exams”. Score each from 1 to 5 for likelihood and impact; their product ranks them. Then do something about the biggest: plan a spike (a short, timeboxed experiment, a few hours at most) for the riskiest unknowns first, so that a fatal problem shows up in week one rather than week five; keep a fallback (“use a small hand-made map”).

Finally, write the pitch: one short paragraph saying who it’s for, what problem they have, what your program does about it, and what’s interesting about building it. Plain, specific words: “walkers who want a route of about two hours from their door” says more than “a seamless, innovative walking experience”. If you can’t write the pitch, you don’t have an idea yet.

Waymark helps people who walk for pleasure plan routes from a map of local footpaths: give it a start, an end or a target time, and it finds a route, estimates how long it will take including the climbing, and keeps a log of walks done. Building it means modelling a path network as a graph, searching it efficiently, storing walks in a database and serving it through a small API.

Key idea

A capstone proves you can build something whole, anchors your portfolio and teaches scoping and finishing. Find ideas in your own life, and avoid ones that are too big, too vague, dependent on things you can’t control, or copies of large products. Compare candidates with a weighted scoring matrix and dealbreakers. Estimate with three-point (PERT) estimates, (o + 4m + p) / 6; totals add, and the total’s spread is the square root of the summed variances. Plan against capacity with a focus factor (about 0.7). Cut with MoSCoW; the MVP is the musts, useful end to end, and if the musts don’t fit, cut the idea. Rank risks by likelihood times impact and spike the biggest first. Write a plain, specific pitch: user, problem, what it does, why it’s interesting to build.

Practice

Exercise10 points
A weighted scoring matrix

Write rank_ideas(ideas, weights, dealbreakers=("feasible", "data")): ideas maps names to dicts of scores (1 to 5) per criterion; weights maps criteria to weights. Each idea’s score is the weighted sum divided by the largest possible weighted sum, as a percentage rounded to 1 place. Return a list of (name, score) from best to worst (ties by name), leaving out any idea that scores 1 on a dealbreaker criterion. Then excluded(ideas, dealbreakers=("feasible", "data")): the sorted names that were left out.

Exercise10 points
Three-point estimates

Write pert(o, m, p): (expected, sd) with expected = (o + 4m + p) / 6 and sd = (p − o) / 6, both rounded to 2 places; raise ValueError("need o <= m <= p") otherwise. plan_total(tasks) for a dict of (o, m, p) tuples: (expected, sd) for the whole plan, where expected times add and the spread is the square root of the summed variances (both 2 places, computed from unrounded values). And chance_within(tasks, hours): the probability (2 places) that the plan takes at most hours, treating the total as normally distributed: 0.5 × (1 + erf((hours − expected) / (sd × √2))).

Exercise15 points
Cutting to fit

Write fit_scope(features, capacity): features is a list of dicts with name, priority ("must", "should", "could" or "wont") and hours. Include every must (if they don’t fit, raise ValueError("musts need N hours") with their total); then go through the shoulds, then the coulds, each group in the given order, adding each one that still fits and skipping any that doesn’t; never include a won’t. Return (included, deferred, hours_used): the included names in that order, the deferred names (everything else) in their original order, and the hours.

Exercise10 points
A risk register

Write rank_risks(risks): risks is a list of (description, likelihood, impact) with both from 1 to 5. Return a list of (description, score, level) from highest score to lowest (ties in the original order), where score = likelihood × impact and level is "high" for 15 or more, "medium" for 8 or more, else "low". Then spikes(risks, budget, hours_each=3): the descriptions of the high risks to investigate first, highest first, as many as budget hours allows at hours_each hours per spike.

Exercise10 points
Six weeks of hours

Write capacity(hours_per_week, weeks, focus=0.7): the usable hours, rounded to 1 place. Then schedule(features, per_week): assign features (a list of (name, hours), in priority order) to weeks starting at week 1, filling each week up to per_week hours before moving on; a feature that doesn’t fit in what’s left of a week starts the next week (and may run over several weeks if it’s bigger than a week). Return a dict from week number to the names that start in that week, and the week in which the last one finishes, as (starts, last_week).

Exercise10 points
Checking a pitch

Write pitch_problems(text): a list of problems with a pitch, in this order, each included only if it applies: "too short" (under 40 words) or "too long" (over 120); "no user" if none of the words for, who or people appears; "vague words: A, B" listing, in alphabetical order, any of innovative, revolutionary, seamless, cutting-edge, leverage, synergy, next-generation, game-changing used (ignoring case); and "long sentence" if any sentence (split on ., ! and ?) has more than 35 words. Words are runs of letters, digits, hyphens and apostrophes.

Bug hunt10 points
Every criterion counts the same

The weights are ignored, so the chess engine (fun, but hard to finish) wins on a raw total: it prints Chess engine. Use the weights, so it prints Recipe box.

Bug hunt10 points
A spread that’s too wide

Adding the tasks’ standard deviations overstates the uncertainty of the whole plan (some tasks run early while others run late). Combine them as the square root of the summed variances, so it prints sd: 5.0.

Bug hunt10 points
Coulds before musts

Sorting the backlog by the priority text puts "could" first and "must" second, alphabetically: it prints ['GPX export', 'route search', 'saved walks', 'phone app']. Sort by the priorities’ real order, so it prints ['route search', 'saved walks', 'GPX export', 'phone app'].

Your capstone today

Do this on your own computer, in a notes file you’ll keep for the whole capstone (a notes/ folder in what will become the project’s repository is ideal).

  1. Ten ideas in ten minutes. Don’t judge yet. Look at your week: what do you, your family, a club or a workplace do by hand, in a spreadsheet, or badly?
  2. Shortlist three and score them with a weighted matrix like the first exercise’s. Write down why each weight is what it is.
  3. For the winner, list every feature you can think of, give each a three-point estimate and a MoSCoW priority, and fit them to your capacity (capacity(your_hours, 6)). If the musts don’t fit, cut until they do.
  4. Five risks, scored, with a spike planned for any that’s high.
  5. The pitch: one paragraph, checked with pitch_problems.

If you can’t choose between two ideas by the end, pick the one you can finish. Tomorrow you’ll turn it into a written specification.

Before you go

Show your pitch to one person who doesn’t program and ask them to tell you, in their own words, what your program will do. If what they say isn’t what you meant, rewrite the pitch. Tomorrow is writing the specification: users, stories, acceptance criteria and the requirements that will tell you when you’re done.
