Week 100, Day 6

Debrief

Four weeks of interview practice end here, with the question that matters: are you ready? Not ready to know everything (nobody is), but ready to walk into a loop with a method for every round, stories for the questions, and evidence that your scores hold up. The Forge reads your loops, your problem log and your story bank and gives a plain answer with its reasons; the bonus produces the one-page interview kit you’ll read the night before a real interview.

By the end of today you can

  • Judge readiness from loop scores, solve rates and prepared stories
  • Name what still needs practice, with reasons
  • Produce a one-page interview kit
  • Review the drills and loops of the last week
  • Close the interview block

Exercise10 points
The easiest way across

A grid of numbers gives the effort of crossing each cell; you start top left and move only right or down. Write least_effort(grid): the smallest total effort to reach the bottom right (counting both ends).

Exercise10 points
Separate parts of the map

Write parts(n, paths): the number of separate parts of a map with junctions 0 to n - 1 and undirected paths, by breadth-first search.

What ready means

Ready isn’t perfect. A sensible bar: your recent loops average at least 3 out of 4 with no round far below; you solve most of your recent practice problems (say 70%) without the answer; and you have at least six stories prepared and timed. If you’re below the bar in one place, that’s what next week’s spare hours are for. And remember that interviews are partly luck: a “no” from one company says little about the next. Keep the log going after the course; it will keep telling you the truth.

Exercise50 points
The readiness report

Write readiness(loops, log, stories). loops is a list of scorecards (round name to criteria to ratings) in date order; log is a list of practice problems (dicts with solved, in date order); stories is the number of prepared, timed stories. A loop’s overall score is the mean of its rounds’ means. Return (lines, verdict):

  1. "loops: N, overall F to L" (first and last overall, 2 decimal places);
  2. "trend: rising" if the last overall is more than 0.25 above the first, "falling" if more than 0.25 below, else "steady";
  3. "weak rounds in the last loop: A, B" (rounds with a mean below 2.5, in order) or "weak rounds in the last loop: none";
  4. "solve rate: P% of N (last 10: Q%)" (whole percentages);
  5. "stories: S of 6".

verdict is "ready to interview" if the last overall is at least 3, no round is weak, the last-10 solve rate is at least 70% and there are at least 6 stories; otherwise "keep practising: " and the failing ones of loop scores, a weak round, solve rate and stories, joined by ", ".

Four weeks of interviews

You’ve sat six mock loops, more than most people sit before their first real one. Look at your first scorecard and your last: what changed most? It’s usually not knowledge but calm: having a method for every round means there’s always a next step to take. That’s worth more than any single problem you learned.

Bonus: one page for the night before

Write interview_kit(patterns, stories, questions): patterns maps a clue to a technique (for example "longest stretch" to "sliding window"), stories is a list of (id, competencies) and questions a list of questions to ask. Return Markdown lines joined by newlines: # Interview kit, a blank line, ## Method, a blank line, the line Clarify, examples, simple, better, code, test, complexity., a blank line, ## Patterns, a blank line, - CLUE: TECHNIQUE per pattern (sorted by clue), a blank line, ## Stories, a blank line, - ID: COMP, COMP per story (in order), a blank line, ## Questions to ask, a blank line, and 1. QUESTION, 2. ... numbered.
